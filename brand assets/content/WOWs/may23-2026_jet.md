## Content

Making something that makes no literal sense and yet is impossible to ignore is harder than it looks. Paul named this explosive Band on the Run track after a Labrador puppy his family had owned, assembled lyrics that he himself has acknowledged do not quite add up to a coherent narrative, and produced one of the most invigorating songs of the 1970s. "Jet" is a reminder that pure creative energy, when it is genuine, does not require a map.

All creative work contains a kind of intelligence that operates below the level of logical argument. When Paul said "Jet" and felt it was right, he was not wrong to trust that instinct even without being able to explain it. The word sounds like what it means. It moves the air when you say it. It lands in the body before the brain has time to ask questions. That is not an accident. That is craft working faster than consciousness.

Justifying every creative choice through rational explanation is a trap that Paul consistently avoided in his most exhilarating work. "Jet" does not justify itself. It simply arrives, opens at full throttle, and dares you to catch your breath. The emotional truth of the song, which is something like joy at its most unrestrained, communicates perfectly even as the literal content remains gloriously elusive.

One of the lessons artists learn eventually, and some never do, is that the audience does not need to understand a song intellectually to feel it completely. "Jet" works because Paul trusted the feeling when it arrived and did not wait for it to make perfect sense before committing to it. In creative work and in life, there are moments when the most productive thing you can do is simply trust the energy and follow it forward.

Reach for the words that feel right even before you can fully explain why. That instinct, followed consistently, is where the best work comes from.
